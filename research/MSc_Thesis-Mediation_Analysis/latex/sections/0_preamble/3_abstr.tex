\prefacesection{Summary}

Mediation analysis methods have been commonly used in the social sciences 
to elucidate the mechanisms through which known causal relationships operate. 
Recently, the causal inference literature has formalized these methods and investigated 
the identification and estimation of various conceptually distinct versions of mediational 
effects. In this dissertation, we explore the definition, identification, and 
interpretation of natural and interventional effects, with a particular focus on the 
issues with extending these effect definitions to multiple mediator mediation analysis
problems. Through an analysis of existing extensions of mediation analysis methods 
to multiple mediator problems, we argue that natural effects cannot be identified in 
such situations while current definitions of interventional effects face interpretational
issues. We propose that a previously suggested variant of interventional effects for 
problems where the treatment is non-manipulable holds promise in multiple mediator 
settings and demonstrate this via an application to analyzing intergenerational social
class mobility in Britain.
